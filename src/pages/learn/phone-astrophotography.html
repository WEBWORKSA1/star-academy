Phone Astrophotography: Shoot the Moon, Stars & Milky Way

You don’t need a big camera to capture the night sky. With a steady support and the right settings, the phone you own can photograph the Moon, constellations, star trails and even the Milky Way.

The minimal kit

  • A stable support — a small tripod with a phone clamp is the single biggest upgrade. A beanbag or a wall works in a pinch.
  • A timer or remote — use the 3–10-second self-timer so touching the screen doesn’t shake the shot.
  • Night mode or a manual/pro mode — built into most modern phones, or available through third-party camera apps that allow manual ISO, shutter speed and focus.
  • RAW capture, if available, for more editing latitude.

Photographing the Moon

The Moon is bright — a sunlit landscape — so the classic mistake is overexposure, which turns it into a white blob. In pro mode, try ISO 50–100 and a shutter speed around 1/250–1/1000 s, then tap on the Moon and lower exposure until craters appear. Avoid digital zoom; use your phone’s optical telephoto lens if it has one, then crop. The most dramatic shots come near the terminator in crescent and quarter phases, or with a foreground silhouette as the Moon rises.

Stars, constellations and the Milky Way

For star fields, the phone must stay perfectly still for seconds at a time.

  1. Mount the phone, turn off the flash, and use the main (widest-aperture) lens.
  2. Night mode: let it run its longest exposure (often 10–30 s on a tripod).
  3. Pro mode starting point: ISO 1600–3200, 10–20 seconds, white balance around 3800–4500 K, focus manually to infinity (or tap on the brightest star and lock).
  4. Keep exposures short enough to avoid trailing — with a wide phone lens, about 15–25 seconds is usually fine.

The Milky Way needs dark skies (Bortle 4 or darker), no Moon, and the right season: the bright core is best from roughly March to October, highest around midnight in mid-summer for the Northern Hemisphere. Check our sky darkness test and new Moon dates.

Star trails and the ISS

Star trails show Earth’s rotation. Point toward Polaris (north) or the south celestial pole for circular trails, shoot continuously for 30–60 minutes with many 10–30-second exposures, then combine them with a free stacking app (look for “lighten” or “max” blending). The International Space Station appears as a bright streak in a 10–30-second exposure — see our ISS guide for pass times.

Shooting through a telescope (digiscoping)

Hold the phone’s lens centered on the eyepiece, or use an inexpensive phone adapter clamp. Start with a low-power eyepiece, focus the telescope by eye first, then fine-tune on the screen. The Moon is the easiest target; Jupiter’s moons and Saturn’s rings are possible on steady nights by recording a short video and extracting the sharpest frames.

Stacking and editing

Stacking combines many short exposures to reduce noise. Popular free desktop tools include Sequator (Windows), Siril (Windows/macOS/Linux) and DeepSkyStacker (Windows). For simple edits on the phone, lower highlights, add a little contrast and clarity, and nudge white balance cooler.

Constellations

Constellations for Beginners: Learn the Sky Season by Season

You don’t need to memorize 88 constellations. Learn a few bright patterns each season, measure the sky with your hand, and star-hop to everything else.

By the Star.Academy Editorial Team · Updated November 2026 · 9 min read ·

Constellations vs asterisms

The sky is divided into 88 constellations — official regions defined by the International Astronomical Union, like countries on a map. Many familiar shapes are actually asterisms: patterns within or across constellations. The Big Dipper is part of Ursa Major; the Summer Triangle spans three constellations. Asterisms are the perfect place to start.

Measure the sky with your hand

Held at arm’s length, your fist covers about 10°, three middle fingers about 5°, and your little finger about 1° — twice the width of the full Moon. Astronomy guides use these measures constantly (“Jupiter is two fists above the horizon”).

Northern Hemisphere, season by season

Winter (Dec–Feb evenings)

Start with Orion: three bright stars in a row form his Belt, with orange Betelgeuse above and blue-white Rigel below. Follow the Belt down-left to Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, and up-right to orange Aldebaran and the Pleiades star cluster. The bright stars around Orion form the Winter Hexagon: Sirius, Procyon, Pollux, Capella, Aldebaran and Rigel. Below the Belt, the misty patch in Orion’s Sword is the Orion Nebula.

Spring (Mar–May)

The Big Dipper rides high in the north. Its two “pointer” stars lead to Polaris, the North Star. Follow the curve of the Dipper’s handle to “arc to Arcturus,” a bright orange star, then “spike to Spica” in Virgo. Look for the backward question mark of Leo marked by Regulus.

Summer (Jun–Aug)

Three bright stars make the Summer Triangle: Vega, Deneb and Altair. Deneb sits at the top of Cygnus, the Northern Cross, which lies along the Milky Way. Low in the south are Scorpius with red Antares and the “Teapot” of Sagittarius, which points toward the center of our galaxy.

Autumn (Sep–Nov)

Find the Great Square of Pegasus, then hop from its corner star along a line of stars in Andromeda to locate the fuzzy Andromeda Galaxy. The W (or M) shape of Cassiopeia lies across from the Big Dipper on the other side of Polaris.

Southern Hemisphere highlights

The Southern Cross (Crux) is small but bright; nearby, the two Pointers (Alpha and Beta Centauri) lead to it. Alpha Centauri is the closest star system to the Sun. Canopus, the second-brightest star, shines in Carina, and the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds — satellite galaxies of the Milky Way — look like detached pieces of the Milky Way from dark sites. Southern observers also get spectacular views of Scorpius and Sagittarius high overhead in winter.

Tools that help

  • A planisphere (rotating star wheel) for your latitude — no batteries, no glare.
  • Our live sky chart, with constellation lines and a time slider.
  • Binoculars to reveal clusters and nebulae along the Milky Way.

Star-hopping: the key skill

Once you know a few anchors, you can “hop” from bright stars to fainter targets using patterns and hand measures. It’s how observers found everything before computerized telescopes — and it’s deeply satisfying. Practice in Night Sky 101, lesson 4.

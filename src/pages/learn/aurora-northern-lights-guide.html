Aurora

Northern & Southern Lights: How to See the Aurora

What causes the aurora, how to read the Kp index and NOAA alerts, how far from the poles it can reach, and how to capture it with the phone in your pocket.

By the Star.Academy Editorial Team · Updated November 2026 · 8 min read ·

What causes the aurora

The Sun constantly streams charged particles into space — the solar wind. When a fast stream or a coronal mass ejection (a billion-ton cloud of solar plasma) reaches Earth and its magnetic field links up with ours, energy pours into Earth’s magnetosphere. Electrons are accelerated down along magnetic field lines and strike oxygen and nitrogen high in the atmosphere, which glow: green and red from oxygen, purple and blue from nitrogen.

This happens in ring-shaped zones around the magnetic poles called auroral ovals. During geomagnetic storms the ovals expand toward the equator, bringing aurora to places that rarely see it.

The Kp index in plain language

The planetary Kp index measures global geomagnetic disturbance on a scale from 0 (quiet) to 9 (extreme), from magnetometer readings every three hours. NOAA’s storm scale maps onto it: G1 (minor) = Kp 5, G2 = Kp 6, G3 = Kp 7, G4 = Kp 8, G5 (extreme) = Kp 9.

KpWhat it means for observers
0–2Aurora mainly at high latitudes (northern Scandinavia, Iceland, northern Canada, Alaska).
3–4Active: good displays at high latitudes; possible low on the horizon at upper-middle latitudes.
5–6Storm: aurora may be seen from places like Scotland, the northern US and southern Canada, and southern New Zealand/Tasmania in the south.
7–9Strong to extreme: aurora can reach mid-latitudes — rare, memorable events.

How to maximize your chances

  • Get dark and look poleward. Face north (or south in the Southern Hemisphere) with a low, unobstructed horizon, away from city lights.
  • Watch around local midnight. Activity often peaks in the hours around magnetic midnight, roughly 10 pm–2 am, but storms can erupt at any time of night.
  • Check the Moon. A bright Moon washes out fainter displays.
  • Use a camera as a detector. Phone night modes and cameras see color the eye misses. Faint aurora often looks gray-white to the eye but vivid green on a 3–10 second exposure.
  • Be patient. Aurora comes in waves; a quiet glow can explode into dancing curtains within minutes.

Photographing aurora with a phone

Use a tripod, night mode (or pro mode with ISO 800–3200 and 2–10 seconds), tap to focus on a bright star or set focus to infinity, and turn off the flash. Include a foreground — trees, a lake, a house — for scale. Bright, fast-moving displays need shorter exposures to keep structure sharp.

Solar cycle and the years ahead

Solar activity follows a roughly 11-year cycle. NASA and NOAA announced in 2024 that the Sun had reached the maximum period of Solar Cycle 25; strong storms remain possible for several years after maximum, so the mid-2020s continue to be a good time for aurora hunting.

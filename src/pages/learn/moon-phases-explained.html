Why the Moon has phases

The Moon makes no light of its own. Half of it is always lit by the Sun — exactly like half of Earth is in daylight right now. What changes over the month is how much of that sunlit half faces us. As the Moon orbits Earth, our viewing angle sweeps from looking at its dark side (new Moon) to looking straight at its lit side (full Moon) and back again.

The full cycle of phases — the synodic month — lasts about 29.5 days. That is a little longer than the 27.3 days the Moon takes to orbit Earth relative to the stars, because Earth is moving around the Sun at the same time and the Moon must travel a bit farther to line up with the Sun again.

Phases are not caused by Earth’s shadow. Earth’s shadow only falls on the Moon during a lunar eclipse, which can happen only at full Moon.

The eight phases

  1. New Moon — the Moon sits roughly between Earth and the Sun and is lost in the Sun’s glare. Best nights for galaxies and the Milky Way.
  2. Waxing crescent — a thin, growing sliver in the western sky after sunset. Look for earthshine: the “dark” part faintly glowing with sunlight reflected off Earth.
  3. First quarter — half-lit, about a week after new. It’s called a quarter because the Moon is a quarter of the way through its orbit.
  4. Waxing gibbous — more than half lit and growing.
  5. Full Moon — the entire face is lit; it rises around sunset and sets around sunrise.
  6. Waning gibbous — shrinking, rising later each night.
  7. Last (third) quarter — half-lit on the opposite side; rises around midnight.
  8. Waning crescent — a thin morning sliver in the east before dawn.

Waxing or waning? In the Northern Hemisphere, a Moon lit on the right is waxing (growing) and one lit on the left is waning. In the Southern Hemisphere it’s reversed, because you are effectively viewing the Moon “upside down.” Near the equator, crescents look more like smiles or frowns.

When each phase rises and sets

Because the Moon moves about 12–13° eastward among the stars each day, it rises on average about 50 minutes later every day (the exact delay varies with season and latitude). That simple fact explains the schedule of the phases:

Supermoons, micromoons and blue moons

The Moon’s orbit is an ellipse. At perigee it comes within roughly 356,000–370,000 km of Earth; at apogee it is about 404,000–406,700 km away. A full Moon near perigee is popularly called a supermoon. Compared with a full Moon near apogee (a “micromoon”), it looks about 14% wider and roughly 30% brighter — noticeable side by side, subtle by eye on any single night.

A blue moon is either the second full Moon in a calendar month or the third of four full Moons in an astronomical season. They occur roughly every two to three years and are not blue — the name is about rarity. Traditional names such as the Harvest Moon or Beaver Moon come from seasonal folklore; our Sky Calendar lists them with every full Moon and flags supermoons automatically.

The best phase for observing

Counter-intuitively, the full Moon is the worst time to look at lunar detail. With the Sun shining straight down, there are no shadows and the surface looks flat and glaring. The best views are along the terminator, the line separating day from night, where low sunlight throws long shadows from crater walls and mountains. Evenings between waxing crescent and a few days after first quarter are ideal, and even 10×50 binoculars reveal dozens of craters.

Two more things to try: catch a crescent Moon with earthshine shortly after sunset, and watch how the terminator moves visibly over just a couple of hours with a telescope.

Tip: the huge-looking Moon on the horizon is an optical illusion. Photograph it on the horizon and again high in the sky — the images are the same size.

FAQ

Why do we always see the same side of the Moon?

The Moon is tidally locked: it rotates exactly once per orbit. Slight wobbles called libration let us see about 59% of its surface over time.

Can you see the Moon during the day?

Yes — on most days. The Moon is above the horizon for about 12 hours of every 24, so it is often visible in daylight, especially around the quarter phases.

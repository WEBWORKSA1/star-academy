How to Choose Your First Telescope (Without Wasting Money)

Aperture, mounts and budgets explained in plain language — plus the beginner mistakes that send so many telescopes straight to the closet.

Most people who are unhappy with their first telescope bought the wrong type for their sky, their storage space or their patience — not a “bad brand.” This guide will help you match the instrument to you. If you’d like a quick answer, try our Telescope Finder.

Aperture is king

A telescope’s most important number is its aperture: the diameter of its main lens or mirror. Light-gathering power grows with the area of that opening, so a 200 mm (8-inch) telescope collects about four times as much light as a 100 mm one. More aperture means fainter galaxies, more stars resolved in clusters and finer planetary detail.

Magnification, by contrast, simply depends on which eyepiece you use: telescope focal length ÷ eyepiece focal length. Any telescope can be pushed to absurd magnifications; the useful maximum is about 2× per millimeter of aperture (≈50× per inch) on a steady night. A box promising “600× power” on a 60 mm telescope is a red flag. Our magnification calculator does the math for you.

The main telescope types

  • Refractors use lenses. They are rugged, need little maintenance and give crisp views of the Moon and planets, but large apertures get expensive quickly.
  • Newtonian reflectors use mirrors, offering the most aperture per dollar. Mirrors occasionally need simple alignment (collimation) — a five-minute skill.
  • Dobsonians are Newtonian reflectors on a simple rocking box mount. Point and look. An 8-inch Dobsonian is the most-recommended first “serious” telescope for visual observing.
  • Catadioptrics (Schmidt-Cassegrains and Maksutovs) fold a long focal length into a short tube — compact, great for planets, often sold on computerized mounts.
  • Smart telescopes are all-in-one imaging devices controlled by a phone app. They find, track and stack images automatically, showing color views of nebulae even from cities, at the cost of not looking through an eyepiece.

Mounts matter as much as optics

A wobbly mount ruins a good telescope. Alt-azimuth mounts move up-down and left-right, which is intuitive. Equatorial mounts are tilted to follow the sky’s rotation, useful for tracking and photography but confusing at first. GoTo mounts point automatically after alignment, and app-guided designs use your phone to show where to push the tube — superb for beginners in light-polluted areas where star-hopping is hard.

Accessories that genuinely help: a red flashlight, a planisphere or sky app, a low-power wide-field eyepiece and a simple reflex finder. Skip bundles of low-quality Barlow lenses and filters at the start.

Beginner mistakes to avoid

  • Buying for magnification instead of aperture and mount stability.
  • Buying too big to carry. The best telescope is the one you’ll actually take outside. Check the weight of each piece and where you will store it.
  • Expecting Hubble colors through the eyepiece. Most galaxies and nebulae look like soft gray glows to the eye — still thrilling, but different from processed photos.
  • Not learning the sky first. A month with binoculars and our Night Sky 101 course makes any telescope far more rewarding.
  • Observing through a window or right after bringing the scope outside. Let it cool to outdoor temperature for 20–45 minutes.

What you will really see

With a typical 130–200 mm telescope: hundreds of lunar craters and mountains, Jupiter’s cloud belts and four bright moons, Saturn’s rings and its moon Titan, the phases of Venus, double stars in contrasting colors, star clusters such as the Pleiades and Hercules Cluster, the Orion Nebula, and — from darker skies — the cores of galaxies like Andromeda and the Whirlpool.

What a meteor shower is

A meteor — a “shooting star” — is a grain of space dust, often smaller than a sand grain, hitting our atmosphere at tens of kilometers per second and burning up around 80–120 km overhead. On any dark night you might see a few random (sporadic) meteors per hour.

A meteor shower happens when Earth passes through a stream of debris left by a comet (or, rarely, an asteroid). Because the particles travel in parallel, they appear to radiate from one point in the sky, the radiant, and the shower is named after the constellation containing it — the Perseids radiate from Perseus, the Geminids from Gemini.

How to watch (and see more)

  1. Go dark. Light pollution can cut what you see by 80% or more. Even a park away from streetlights helps; a rural site is far better.
  2. Watch after midnight. For most showers, rates climb after local midnight as your side of Earth turns to face the direction of Earth’s orbit. The Geminids are the main exception, active from mid-evening.
  3. Give your eyes 20–30 minutes. No phone screens (or use a red screen filter).
  4. Lie back and take in the whole sky. Don’t stare at the radiant; meteors appear everywhere, and those 30–50° away from it often have longer trails.
  5. No telescope or binoculars. Meteors are too fast and too spread out. Your eyes are the perfect instrument.
  6. Dress warmly and be patient. Meteors come in bursts. Plan to watch for at least an hour.

ZHR and realistic expectations

The Zenithal Hourly Rate is a standardized number: how many meteors one observer would see per hour under a perfectly dark sky (limiting magnitude 6.5) with the radiant directly overhead. Real conditions are rarely ideal. From a suburban site, or with the radiant low, or with a bright Moon, expect a fraction of the ZHR — perhaps 10–30% of it. That is why the Moon’s phase on peak night matters so much.

Photographing meteors

Use a camera or phone on a tripod with a wide lens, manual focus at infinity, high ISO (1600–3200) and exposures of 10–25 seconds, shooting continuously for an hour or more. Point about 45° away from the radiant. Most frames will be empty — the reward is the one that isn’t. Then enter it in our monthly contest!

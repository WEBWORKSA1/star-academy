Eclipses

Solar & Lunar Eclipses: Types, Safety and How to Watch

Eclipses are among nature’s greatest spectacles. Learn the difference between total, annular, partial and penumbral eclipses, how to protect your eyes, and how to photograph them.

By the Star.Academy Editorial Team · Updated November 2026 · 8 min read ·

⚠️ The one rule

Never look at the Sun — directly or through binoculars, telescopes or cameras — without certified solar filters. The only safe naked-eye moment is during the brief totality of a total solar eclipse. Lunar eclipses are completely safe to watch.

Solar eclipses

A solar eclipse happens at new Moon when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun and its shadow falls on Earth. They don’t happen every month because the Moon’s orbit is tilted about 5° to Earth’s, so most new Moons pass above or below the Sun.

  • Total — the Moon completely covers the Sun along a narrow path. The sky darkens, stars and planets appear, and the Sun’s pearly corona is revealed. Totality lasts from seconds to about seven minutes at most.
  • Annular — the Moon is near apogee and too small to cover the Sun, leaving a brilliant “ring of fire.” Eye protection is needed throughout.
  • Partial — the Moon covers only part of the Sun. Everyone outside the central path of a total or annular eclipse sees a partial eclipse.
  • Hybrid — rare eclipses that appear annular in some places and total in others.

Lunar eclipses

A lunar eclipse happens at full Moon when Earth passes between the Sun and Moon and Earth’s shadow falls on the Moon. Anyone on the night side of Earth can see it.

  • Total — the Moon enters Earth’s dark inner shadow (umbra) completely and turns copper-red, lit by sunlight filtered through Earth’s atmosphere — every sunrise and sunset on Earth at once. Often called a “blood moon.”
  • Partial — only part of the Moon enters the umbra.
  • Penumbral — the Moon passes only through Earth’s faint outer shadow; the dimming is subtle.

How to watch a solar eclipse safely

  1. Eclipse glasses or handheld viewers that meet the ISO 12312-2 international standard, bought from reputable suppliers. Inspect for scratches or holes; supervise children.
  2. Pinhole projection — a hole in a card projects a tiny image of the Sun onto a surface. The gaps between leaves do the same, making dozens of crescent suns on the ground.
  3. Telescopes and binoculars only with a proper solar filter on the front (sky end). Never use eclipse glasses behind an unfiltered telescope or camera lens — the concentrated light destroys the glasses and your eye in an instant.
  4. Sunglasses, smoked glass, exposed film or CDs are not safe.

Photographing eclipses

For partial phases, put a certified solar filter over the phone or camera lens. During totality only, remove it to capture the corona — then put it back as soon as the first bead of sunlight returns. Lunar eclipses need no filter: use a tripod, and expect longer exposures as the Moon darkens during totality.

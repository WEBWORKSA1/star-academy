Satellites

How to See the International Space Station From Your Backyard

The ISS is one of the brightest objects in the night sky and passes over most of the populated world. Here’s when, where and how to spot it — no equipment needed.

By the Star.Academy Editorial Team · Updated November 2026 · 6 min read ·

What you are looking at

The International Space Station orbits roughly 400 km above Earth at about 28,000 km/h, circling the planet about every 90 minutes — around 15–16 orbits a day. Its large solar arrays reflect sunlight so well that it can rival Venus in brightness. Because its orbit is tilted 51.6° to the equator, it passes over almost everywhere between about 51° north and south latitude, and is visible from even farther north and south on favorable passes.

Why you can only see it around dusk and dawn

The ISS doesn’t make its own light. You see it when you are in darkness but the station, high above, is still in sunlight. That happens for a couple of hours after sunset or before sunrise. In the middle of the night it is usually inside Earth’s shadow and invisible. Visible passes come in batches lasting several days, separated by gaps of a week or more when passes happen in daylight.

How to find a pass

  1. Check a pass prediction for your location: NASA’s Spot the Station (with email/text alerts) or Heavens-Above. Watch the live ground track on our Live Space page.
  2. Note three things: the time, where it appears (e.g. “10° above WNW”) and its maximum height in degrees.
  3. Measure degrees with your hand at arm’s length: a fist covers about 10°, three fingers about 5°. 90° is straight overhead.
  4. Be outside a few minutes early, facing the “appears” direction.

Passes with a maximum height above about 40° are the most impressive. Low passes skim the horizon and may be blocked by trees or buildings.

What it looks like

A steady, bright, white point gliding smoothly across the sky — faster than an airplane at altitude, with no blinking lights, and taking from one to about six minutes to cross. Often it fades out partway across the sky as it enters Earth’s shadow — a beautiful moment to watch.

Photographing the ISS

Put a phone or camera on a tripod, use a wide view, and take a long exposure of 10–30 seconds (pro mode or a long-exposure app). The station records as a bright streak across the stars. Take several overlapping exposures and stack them for a full arc. Through a telescope, skilled imagers can even capture the station’s shape — a fun challenge for later.

Other satellites to spot

On any dark evening, watch for slower, fainter moving points — other satellites. Trains of newly launched communication satellites can look like a string of pearls shortly after launch. Learn more in Night Sky 101, lesson 9.

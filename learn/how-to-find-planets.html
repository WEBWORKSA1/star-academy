Planets

How to Find Planets in the Night Sky Tonight

Planets are the easiest showpieces in the sky — once you know where to look. Here’s how to spot each one with your eyes, binoculars and a small telescope.

By the Star.Academy Editorial Team · Updated November 2026 · 8 min read ·

In this guide
  1. Three tricks to tell planets from stars
  2. Planet by planet
  3. When planets are best placed
  4. What you’ll see with binoculars and telescopes

The quickest way to know which planets are up is our live Tonight’s Sky page: it lists each planet’s best viewing time, height and direction for your location. This guide teaches you to recognize them yourself.

Three tricks to tell planets from stars

  1. Follow the ecliptic. All planets travel close to the Sun’s yearly path, the ecliptic, which runs through the zodiac constellations. Imagine the arc the Sun and Moon follow across your sky — planets sit near that line.
  2. The twinkle test. Stars are points of light and twinkle strongly, especially low down. Planets show tiny disks, so their light is steadier.
  3. They move. Over days and weeks, planets shift against the background stars — the ancient Greek word planētēs means “wanderer.”

Planet by planet

Venus — the brilliant “evening/morning star”

Nothing in the night sky except the Moon outshines Venus. Because its orbit lies inside Earth’s, it never strays far from the Sun — at most about 47° — so you’ll find it in the west after sunset or the east before sunrise, often mistaken for an airplane landing light. A small telescope shows its phases, just like the Moon’s.

Mercury — the elusive one

Mercury stays within about 28° of the Sun and is visible only low in bright twilight for a couple of weeks around each greatest elongation. Our calendar lists the next elongations; look low over a flat horizon.

Mars — the orange wanderer

Mars varies dramatically in brightness. Around opposition, which happens about every 26 months, it is bright and orange-red and shows surface markings and a polar cap in a telescope. Far from opposition it is a modest reddish star.

Jupiter — the king

Brilliant and creamy-white, Jupiter is visible for most of the year. Hold binoculars steady and you’ll see up to four tiny “stars” in a line beside it: the Galilean moons Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, whose positions change nightly. A telescope shows dark cloud belts and, at times, the Great Red Spot.

Saturn — the jewel

Saturn shines with a steady golden light. Even a small telescope at about 30–50× reveals its rings — a sight that converts people to astronomy for life. The tilt of the rings changes over a roughly 15-year cycle; they appeared edge-on in 2025 and are slowly opening again.

Uranus and Neptune

Uranus is near the limit of naked-eye visibility under dark skies and is easy in binoculars if you know where to look; it shows a tiny blue-green disk in a telescope. Neptune requires binoculars or a telescope and a finder chart.

When planets are best placed

  • Outer planets (Mars to Neptune) are best around opposition, when Earth passes between them and the Sun: they rise at sunset, are up all night and are at their closest and brightest.
  • Inner planets (Mercury and Venus) are best around greatest elongation, when they appear farthest from the Sun.
  • Planets near the Sun in the sky (around conjunction) are lost in its glare for weeks.
  • Higher is better: views through less atmosphere are sharper. Try to observe a planet when it is highest, usually near when it crosses due south (north in the Southern Hemisphere).

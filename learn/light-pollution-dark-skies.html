Dark Skies

Light Pollution & Dark Skies: How to Find Truly Dark Places

Most of humanity can no longer see the Milky Way from home. Learn how light pollution works, how dark your sky really is, where to find better skies — and what you can still enjoy from the city.

By the Star.Academy Editorial Team · Updated November 2026 · 7 min read ·

The problem in one paragraph

Artificial light scattered by the atmosphere creates skyglow, the orange-gray dome over towns that drowns out faint stars. A landmark 2016 world atlas of artificial sky brightness estimated that more than 80% of the world’s population — and more than 99% of people in the United States and Europe — live under light-polluted skies, and that about a third of humanity can no longer see the Milky Way. Citizen-science measurements from the Globe at Night program suggest that the brightness of the night sky has kept rising quickly over the past decade.

The Bortle scale

Amateur astronomers rate sky darkness on the 9-step Bortle scale:

ClassSkyWhat you see
1–2Excellent / truly darkZodiacal light, airglow, Milky Way so bright it can cast faint shadows
3–4RuralDetailed Milky Way, some glow on the horizon
5–6SuburbanMilky Way faint or only overhead, gray sky
7–9Urban to inner cityOnly bright stars, planets and the Moon

How to find darker skies near you

  • Use a light-pollution map such as lightpollutionmap.info to find the nearest darker zones — often 30–90 minutes’ drive from a city.
  • Look for certified places. DarkSky International has certified more than 200 International Dark Sky Places — parks, reserves and communities committed to protecting the night.
  • Get high and get away from the city’s direction. Hills and plateaus put you above haze; position yourself so city glow is behind you.
  • Time it with the Moon. The darkest week is centered on the new Moon — see the calendar.
  • Stay safe. Tell someone where you’re going, scout the site in daylight and respect private land.

What you can still enjoy from the city

Light pollution hides faint, diffuse objects, but many highlights cut right through it: the Moon in all its phases, every bright planet, colorful double stars like Albireo, bright clusters such as the Pleiades, the International Space Station, and lunar and solar eclipses (with proper solar filters for the Sun). App-guided telescopes and smart telescopes also help city observers find and image faint targets.

How to help protect the night

Use outdoor lights only where and when needed, choose fully shielded fixtures that point down, prefer warm-colored LEDs (around 3000 K or lower), and support local dark-sky ordinances. Better lighting saves energy, helps wildlife and lets everyone see the stars again.
